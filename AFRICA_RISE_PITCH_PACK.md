# Part II — The Case

## 5. The problem

### 5.1 The wrong race, and the right one

The model race — who builds the biggest AI — is being decided between the United States and China. Senegal will not win it, and does not need to. But the layer that protects everything AI touches is still unbuilt in Africa, and that layer decides whether African institutions own their security or rent it. The dependency is already compounding:

1. **Regulation just got real.** Senegal's National Assembly passed a new digital and cybersecurity bill (No. 25/2026) on 21 August 2026, after breaches hit the Treasury (May 2026), the Court of Audit (June 2026), and the tax authority DGID (October 2025).[^1] The bill sets out local hosting of public-sector data, a National Cybersecurity Authority, a national CERT, sectoral CERTs and SOCs, and lets regulators require cyber-insurance for exposed sectors.
2. **The tools regulated entities need to comply are foreign, expensive, and often non-compliant with the bill's own data-residency requirement.** A scanning platform that ships your findings to a US or European SaaS backend is itself a compliance liability under a bill written specifically to stop that.
3. **The AI layer makes this worse, not better.** Every "AI-assisted" security tool on the market routes prompts — which inevitably contain client names, IPs, and credentials — straight to OpenAI or Anthropic's servers. For a bank or a ministry under a local-hosting mandate, that is a second compliance failure stacked on the first. It also means the country's security posture is, quite literally, dependent on infrastructure it does not control.
4. **The continent's cybersecurity spend is tiny and growing from a low base** — which is the opportunity, not a disqualifier (see §7). Nobody has built the compliant, local, first-mover platform yet.

The deeper risk is not just that foreign tools are expensive. It is that if the cybersecurity layer protecting African digital infrastructure is entirely foreign-owned and foreign-hosted, African governments and institutions have effectively outsourced their own digital sovereignty to the same global powers they cannot out-build.

That is the gap Ultorn exists to close.

### 5.2 The sports version: speed is the new attack surface

The same structural problem shows up in African sport in a sharper, more human form.

A club's marketing lead can now ship a ticketing platform in a weekend. A federation's operations manager can launch a fan app with a prompt. A founder can build an academy portal before the season starts. That is a genuine superpower — and it compresses the distance between "idea" and "exposed production system" to days.

AI writes code that looks right. It hardcodes keys to make the demo work. It disables TLS when the certificate fails. It trusts user input. It ships `eval`, opens CORS to the world, and leaves the admin panel on the public internet. The code ships fast. The doors stay open.

And it is not only code. Staff paste athlete passports, medical notes, minors' records, and fan payment data into foreign AI models to write reports, summarize contracts, or draft communications. The data leaves the building. Nobody logs it. Nobody knows where it went.

Matchday makes it worse: there is a hard deadline, a full stadium, a sponsor watching, and no security team to call. A ticketing outage is not an IT incident — it is a revenue event, a reputational event, and, when minors' data is involved, a safeguarding event.

Sports is not a side quest. It is the most visible proof that the trust layer has to be built before the next season, not after the next breach.

### 5.3 Senegal, specifically — the numbers, not the abstraction

This is not a hypothetical risk. It is the documented pattern of the last eighteen months:

- **3,794 cybercrime offenses recorded by the National Police in 2025** (vs. 3,902 in 2024), with 257 people brought before the courts — mostly online scams, data breaches, and unauthorized computer-system access.[^3] Police figures only capture what is reported, so the real number is higher.
- **A dark-web actor calling itself "The Green Blood" claimed a breach of the Department for the Automation of Records (DAF) on 5 February 2026**, claiming 139 TB of data. The incident reportedly forced a temporary suspension of national ID card production — a government service stopped, not just a data leak.[^4] The 139 TB figure is unconfirmed by Senegalese authorities. Say "claimed."
- **The Treasury (May 2026), the Court of Audit (June 2026), and the tax authority DGID (October 2025)** — the three incidents that directly drove Bill No. 25/2026 through the National Assembly.[^1]
- **In December 2025, Interpol-backed authorities stopped a $7.9M business-email-compromise attack** against a major oil company operating in Senegal — attackers impersonated executives inside the company's own messaging systems to try to authorize a fraudulent transfer.[^5] This is the sophistication level already in play against Senegalese enterprise, not a theoretical future threat.
- **Mobile money is both the country's biggest fintech success and its most-targeted attack surface.** A joint Interpol operation across 16 African countries including Senegal (December 2025–January 2026) led to 651 arrests for mobile-money and related fraud;[^6] a 2023 prosecuted investment-scam ring moved over 4 billion FCFA through Orange Money and Wave from 1,811 victims.[^7] To be fair to the platforms: Wave has publicly claimed a fraud rate of ~0.00056% of transaction volume — the exposure is overwhelmingly in social-engineering and scam networks around the platforms, not platform compromise itself.[^7]
- **Interpol estimates African cyberattack losses reached at least $5 billion in 2025** continent-wide.[^5] Senegal's incidents above are its share of that number, not separate from it.
- **The honest gap, and the opportunity in it:** as of the most recent reporting, Senegal still has no fully operational national cybersecurity agency or national CERT — Bill No. 25/2026 sets out to create them, but stand-up takes time.[^4] That is the window: the institutions that will set accreditation criteria and enforce the regime do not exist yet. A platform already built to their stated obligations, before they are seated, is positioned to shape — not just comply with — what "compliant" ends up meaning in practice.

### 5.4 One sentence to carry into every conversation

If the security layer protecting African digital infrastructure is entirely foreign-owned and foreign-hosted, African institutions have not bought security. They have rented dependence.

## 6. The solution

### 6.1 What Ultorn is

**Ultorn** is a defensive cybersecurity platform: AI-driven vulnerability detection, continuous threat monitoring, AI-assisted code review, findings management, and — the part that maps directly onto the new bill — a compliance module that maps every finding to the specific obligations it triggers (CERT notification windows, sector-specific duties, local-hosting status).

Our approach is **secure AI integration**: use the best available models, but minimize exposure of sensitive data to any external provider by design.

Ultorn is how we win the race we can win: not a bigger model, but a sovereign defense layer over everything AI touches — the part of the stack no foreign vendor should own for us.

### 6.2 Five things it does

**1. It finds exposure.**
Scans domains, IPs, and URLs with Nuclei, Nmap, and full HTTP evidence collection — headers, banners, titles, response bodies, service detection, template matches. No exploitation. Just the truth about what is visible.

**2. It watches.**
Continuous monitors check ticketing, streaming, membership, and results endpoints for outages, configuration drift, leaked credentials, access anomalies, and policy violations. Scheduled scans run hourly through monthly. Alerts can be acknowledged, resolved, or marked false positive, and signed webhooks push them to Slack, Teams, or a SIEM.

**3. It proves.**
Every scan, finding, review, AI query, and administrative action goes into an immutable audit log. Critical and high findings require a named analyst's attestation before they close. Evidence attachments — screenshots, raw scanner output — live on the finding. When a sponsor, federation, insurer, or auditor asks what happened, the record exists.

**4. It complies.**
Findings map to Bill No. 25/2026: a draft article reference, the notification window, and the sector-specific obligation where a sector pack exists. The engine maps incident notification, vulnerability disclosure, local hosting, security audits, and AI governance — and the sports sector pack adds operational guidance for clubs, federations, and academies: matchday availability, athlete and minor safeguarding, fan and payment data, federation evidence, and AI-in-scouting. The same engine is designed to take banking, telecom, and government packs; those are roadmap, not shipped. The output is a plain-language executive report and a Markdown export a board can actually read.

**5. It protects the AI era in two directions.**
AI Guard sanitizes every prompt before it is allowed near an external model: client names, IPs, credentials, API keys, JWTs, emails, phone numbers, SSNs, and credit-card numbers are stripped. If a prompt still looks sensitive after sanitization, it never leaves the building — a local responder answers instead. An Ollama-class open-source model is a drop-in provider for clients who want zero external calls. Every query is logged immutably: provider, model, whether it was sanitized — never the raw content.

The second direction is the code scanner. It catches the fingerprints of vibe coding — hardcoded secrets, dangerous `eval`/`exec`, disabled TLS verification, insecure defaults — before they reach fans, patients, or citizens.

### 6.3 Why it is defensible, not just "a scanner"

- **Sovereignty by architecture, not by policy document.** The sanitization boundary is code. The local fallback is code. The audit log is code. It does not depend on a vendor's promise or a data-processing agreement.
- **Self-hostable; findings and scan evidence stay in the client's infrastructure.** SQLite for single-node, Postgres for scale, deployable entirely on-premises or in a Senegal-based data center — precisely what Bill No. 25/2026 sets out for public-sector data. AI Guard strips sensitive data before any external call, and a local-model option removes external calls entirely.
- **AI agents assist under a human-review gate; they do not act alone.** Findings detection, triage, and code review are AI-assisted. Critical and high findings still require a named analyst's sign-off, and nothing autonomously exploits anything. That is a deliberate choice, not a limitation — it is what lets this be sold into banks and government, where "an AI did it" is not an acceptable answer to a regulator.
- **Operational completeness.** Role-based access control enforced end to end, API-key access for programmatic and CI integration, evidence attachments, recurring scan schedules, finding deduplication across rescans, an operator console, and a working sports demo tenant. This is a working product today, not a slide.

### 6.4 What it is not — say this before someone else does

- **Not offensive.** No exploitation, no lateral movement, no data exfiltration beyond banner/header/body evidence.
- **Not autonomous.** No AI closes a critical finding or takes an action on its own.
- **Not a foundation model.** We do not compete with OpenAI or Anthropic on model quality. We compete on what is allowed to reach them, and on what stays home.
- **Not a compliance certificate.** The platform maps findings to obligations and produces evidence. It does not replace a lawyer, an auditor, or the regulator's own accreditation. Say that plainly; it builds more trust than overclaiming.
- **Not finished enterprise software.** It is a working MVP with a full operator console. It has not been battle-tested at multi-replica volume, and it has not had an independent security audit. No audit is claimed. We will commission an independent penetration test after the first pilot.

# Part IV — Pitch Defense

## 14. Anticipated questions and how to answer them

### On the market

**Q: The African cybersecurity market is less than a billion dollars. Why should I care?**
A: Because it is growing at 13%+ CAGR off a bill that passed barely two months ago, and because "small today" is exactly the description of every market a first-mover wants to own before it is not small. The question is not the size of the market today — it is who is ready for Senegal's new bill when the next ministry RFP goes out. Right now, nobody else is selling that combination.

**Q: Isn't this too niche — one country, one law?**
A: Senegal is the beachhead, not the ceiling. The compliance-mapping engine is a rules layer over a generic scanning platform. Mapping it to Côte d'Ivoire's or Nigeria's data-protection regime is a configuration change, not a rebuild. We sequence Senegal first because it is home, the bill is new, and we can move faster than anyone importing a platform from outside.

**Q: Why pitch this at a sports summit?**
A: Because sport is where the problem is most visible and most human: minors' records, athlete passports, fan payments, and a hard matchday deadline. It is also where Africa Rise already has distribution. The same platform sells to banks, telecoms, and government — sports is the proof, not the ceiling.

### On the product

**Q: What stops a client from just using Qualys or a free open-source scanner?**
A: Nothing stops them using the scanning engine. What those tools do not do is keep the findings, the AI analysis, and the compliance mapping inside the client's own jurisdiction — which the bill passed in August 2026 sets out for public-sector data in Senegal. We are not competing on scan quality alone. We are competing on being the only option that does not create a second compliance problem while solving the first.

**Q: AI security tools are everywhere now — what is actually defensible about yours?**
A: Most "AI security" products are a prompt wrapper around someone else's model with no sanitization step. Ours strips PII, credentials, and IPs before anything leaves the sanitization boundary, refuses to send sensitive content externally at all, and logs every query immutably so a bank's compliance officer can prove, after the fact, exactly what did and did not leave the building. That is a governance feature, not a model feature — much harder to copy than a better prompt.

**Q: You say "sovereignty," but you still use Anthropic and OpenAI under the hood — isn't that a contradiction?**
A: No — it is the actual strategy, not a compromise. We do not believe Senegal wins by refusing to use the world's best models. We believe it wins by controlling what reaches them. Every prompt is sanitized before it is allowed near an external model: names, IPs, credentials, PII stripped. Anything still sensitive after sanitization never leaves at all; a local responder handles it instead. An open-source model is a drop-in option for clients who want zero external calls ever. We use foreign AI the way a bank uses a foreign cloud provider for non-sensitive workloads: selectively, with a hard boundary, never for what actually matters. That boundary — not which model we call — is the sovereignty.

**Q: You just said you can't compete with the US or China. Why should anyone back a company that admits it can't win?**
A: Because we are not running their race. In cybersecurity, the moat is not model quality — it is jurisdiction, data residency, local presence, and local accountability. We use their models; we own the boundary around them. That boundary is a business they have no incentive to build for Senegal and every incentive to sell us instead. "Can't out-build them" is not a weakness; it is the strategy.

**Q: Is this actually secure, or is this a startup with a security story that has never been audited?**
A: No independent security audit yet. We have built with RBAC, rate-limited auth, immutable audit logging, generated secrets rather than committed ones, and a sanitization boundary that is code rather than a policy document. We will commission an independent penetration test after the first pilot and publish the result. We will not claim an audit we have not had.

**Q: Why not just use a web agency or a free scanner for a club?**
A: A free scanner tells you something is open. It does not tell the club what to do before matchday, does not watch the endpoint while the stadium fills, does not map the finding to a statutory notification window, and does not give the federation one evidence trail across every club. The real alternative for most clubs is doing nothing. We are trying to make "nothing" the more expensive option.
